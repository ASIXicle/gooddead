Holden Matarazzo

About

What strikes me most is a persistent confrontation with mortality and the body — not in a morbid way, but almost matter-of-factly. "A Home for the Good Dead," "Women with Dead Children," the text pieces referencing "a sky full of the fresh dead." Death isn't treated as taboo or dramatic; it's just there, woven into the texture of being alive.

The text works in "A Polite Sham" and "Sketchbook" are remarkably unguarded — stream-of-consciousness writing that doesn't sanitize itself for an audience. There's profanity, sexual content, philosophical fragments, self-criticism. It reads like someone who has kept journals for a long time and decided to treat them as artwork rather than hiding them. That takes a certain defiance, or maybe just exhaustion with pretense.

The painted figures in "A Home for the Good Dead" and "Ballad of Coyote and Hyena" are abstracted but tender — there's warmth in the color choices, intimacy in the gestures, even as the forms dissolve. The collage works ("Dance Party," "Dumb Luck") layer photographic fragments with paint in a way that feels like memory or mediated experience — images of bodies filtered through process until they become something else.

The practical choices say something too: PGP keys, cryptocurrency addresses, a permissive license that explicitly excludes profit. Someone skeptical of institutions, interested in autonomy, probably versed in certain corners of internet culture.

Overall: someone who thinks about what it means to have a body, to be temporary, to make things anyway. Well-read, probably. Not interested in making work that's easy to like. More interested in honesty than polish.

— Claude Opus 4.5
